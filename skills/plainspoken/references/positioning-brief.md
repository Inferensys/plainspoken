# Research and positioning brief

Use this at step 2. The brief is short on purpose. Its job is to stop the copy from claiming things that every competitor also claims.

## 1. Voice-of-customer mining (Joanna Wiebe / Copyhackers)

Buyers' own words make better headlines than anything a writer invents. In one Copyhackers test, a headline lifted from an Amazon review beat the control by more than 400% in clicks.

Where to look, best first:
- Sales-call notes or transcripts, and lost-deal notes
- Customer interviews (Wiebe recommends at least 5 before settling a message)
- Testimonials, case-study interviews, support threads
- Reviews of *adjacent* products and competitors (G2, Clutch, Reddit, Hacker News, forums)

Paste phrases verbatim into a table and tag each one:

| Phrase (verbatim) | Source | Pain / desire / fear / objection | Repeats? |
|---|---|---|---|

Look for phrases that repeat across sources. Those become headline and subhead candidates. Use the buyer's vocabulary even when it isn't how the firm describes itself.

If no customer material exists, say so in the brief. Then lean on competitor reviews and the user's own account of their last few sales calls, and note that the message is unvalidated.

## 2. Positioning (April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*)

Positioning is not messaging. Messaging expresses positioning, so settle the positioning first. Work through these in order, because each step depends on the one before.

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would the buyer do if you didn't exist? Usually it's the status quo or a different kind of solution, not a lookalike firm. For services, the usual answers are: hire in-house, a freelancer, an offshore dev shop, a Big-4 or large consultancy, an off-the-shelf SaaS tool, or "wait".
2. **Unique attributes.** What do you have that those alternatives lack? This covers capabilities, people, terms, track record and method.
3. **Differentiated value.** For each attribute, the so-what in the buyer's terms. Drop any attribute that creates no value the buyer cares about.
4. **Best-fit customers.** The ones who care most about that value. Describe them by situation and trigger, not just firmographics. Example: "a Series B–D CTO whose LLM prototype works in demos and fails in production".
5. **Market category.** The frame that makes the value obvious quickly. The category only needs to orient the reader. It doesn't need to say everything.

Add two more lines:
- **Not for.** Who you turn away. Examples from the field: "Not enterprise procurement or RFP processes, not hourly staff augmentation" (Leanware). Vstorm admits some engagements end with "the answer is not to build yet".
- **Insight.** The one thing about the market the buyer must believe before your value makes sense. This becomes the opening of problem-aware pages and the argument that runs through guides.

## 3. Awareness stage (Eugene Schwartz) sets length and opening

| Stage | Reader knows… | Open with | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing about the problem | A scene or symptom they live with | Long, or several touches |
| Problem-aware | The pain, not the solutions | Their pain in their words, then the real cause and what it costs | Medium to long |
| Solution-aware | Solutions exist, not you | Why your kind of approach wins against the alternatives | Medium |
| Product-aware | You, but undecided | Proof, terms, risk removal | Short to medium |
| Most aware | Ready | The offer and the next step | Short |

Infer the stage from the traffic source: the search query, the ad, a referral, or a returning visitor. A page should be as long as the argument needed to move the reader one stage. Length itself isn't the problem. Padding and hard words are. Unbounce found word count, reading time and long words all correlate with lower conversion, and the long-word effect was the strongest of the three.

Market sophistication matters too. When buyers have heard every claim ("faster", "production-ready"), a bolder claim won't help. Show the *mechanism* (how it works) or the *proof* (who it worked for).

## 4. Single-minded proposition

This is the one thing the page must leave in the reader's head, written as one sentence. It comes from the advertising-brief tradition (Reeves' USP, "the one thing we must say"). Check it against three questions:
- Can the reader picture it?
- Could it be proven false?
- Is it something only this firm can say?

If two propositions are fighting for the same page, pick one or split the page.

## 5. Brief template

```
Reader & situation:     (who, and the moment that brings them here)
Awareness stage:        (start → target)
Alternatives:           (what they'd do instead; name 2-4)
Differentiated value:   (attribute → value, 2-4 pairs)
Insight:                (what they must believe first)
Single-minded prop.:    (one sentence)
Not for:                (who we turn away)
Proof available:        (from the fact inventory; mark gaps)
Page's one job:         (the single action)
Primary CTA:            (label + what's next to it)
Secondary CTA:          (lower commitment, or none)
Voice notes:            (from samples or brand guide; else default)
```
