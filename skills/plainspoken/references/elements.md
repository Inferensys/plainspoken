# Element playbook with real examples

The examples come from live sites reviewed in October 2026. Each is quoted briefly so you can learn the move, not lift the line. Never reuse another firm's wording in client copy.

## 1. Hero headline

The headline has to work on its own: about five times as many people read it as read the body (Ogilvy). Julian Shapiro's test: the visitor should know what you sell from the headline alone, plus one hook. The hook can be a bold claim, or the biggest objection handled.

| Weak (seen in the wild) | Why it fails | Stronger pattern |
|---|---|---|
| "AI Solutions for Modern Businesses" | Any firm could say it; nothing to picture | State the offer and the outcome in the buyer's words |
| "Intelligent Analysis, Decision & Execution" | Stacked abstract nouns in Title Case | One concrete claim, in sentence case |
| "Your go-to AI Partner" | A category plus a cliché | Name the job you do and for whom |
| "Software that just works" | True of no one, and claimed by everyone | Add the mechanism, or cut the line |

Patterns that work:
- **A situation-plus-outcome promise you can check.** Vstorm: "we take one real workflow from strategy to a production system your team owns." Tribe AI promises it doesn't stop "until it's in production."
- **Name the buyer in the frame.** Superside calls itself "your creative team's creative team." That positions it as extra capacity, which removes the "will they replace us" fear.
- **Name the alternative and its trade-off, fairly.** Plausible calls itself a "privacy-friendly Google Analytics alternative": the reader knows what it replaces and what it gives up. Describe the alternative neutrally. Don't disparage it, or the reader for having chosen it (see `ai-tells.md` §3).
- **A point of view.** Test Double: "Software problems are human problems."

Write 5–10 headline candidates. Run each through Harry Dry's three tests: can the reader picture it, could it be proven false, could a competitor say it word for word. Present the best 2–3, with the angle of each.

## 2. Subhead

The subhead says how the headline is true: the mechanism, the scope and one credibility anchor. It's the right place for insider vocabulary such as evals, "run agents in production" or a named framework. CTOs read correct jargon as a sign the writer is a peer.

Good shape: `[What we do] + [how/with whom] + [what you keep or get]`.
Example: "Senior engineers embed with your team for six to twelve weeks. You own the code and the evals." That's an illustrative shape. Fill it only with the client's real numbers.

## 3. "Is it for me" situations

Name three or four situations the reader is in, written the way they'd describe them:
- Vstorm: "You have a plan — nothing is in production yet" / "You have an AI team — delivery is stuck"
- Test Double: "Thinking about a big rewrite?" / "Wasting too much on infrastructure?" / "Not sure what you need?"

The last option ("Not sure what you need?") catches undecided visitors without forcing a choice. Each situation should link to the most relevant proof or service.

Add an exclusion line nearby. A firm that turns some business away makes the rest of its claims easier to believe.

## 4. Section headings

People who scan often read only the headings, which NN/g calls the "layer-cake" pattern. So the headings should form the page's argument, and the first two words of each should carry the meaning.

| Label (weak) | Claim (stronger) |
|---|---|
| Our Approach | Evals before features |
| Why Choose Us | [Specific differentiator as a sentence] |
| Key Features | [Outcome the features add up to] |
| Introduction | [The reader's question, answered] |
| Case Studies | [Best result as the heading, e.g. "2 hours to 3 minutes per workflow"] |

Vercel structures sections around one customer each: "Notion powers millions of agent conversations daily on Vercel." For a services firm, pair one proof with each service.

## 5. Body copy

- Put the main point in the first two sentences. Readers read about 20–28% of a page's words.
- Write one idea per paragraph. Use bullets for steps and parallel items, and vary how the bullets open, since readers skip openings that repeat.
- Illustrate instead of asserting (Shleyner, Ogilvy). Rolls-Royce never called the car quiet. It said that at 60 mph the loudest noise is the electric clock.
- Mention table-stakes items (security, support, integrations) in one compact line. Spend the words on the differentiators.
- Cut every sentence about the firm that doesn't answer "so what for me?" (Handley).

## 6. Proof placement

Put proof directly after the claim it supports, in the same section. Don't open with proof before the claim is clear (Wiebe), and don't bury it all in one testimonials block (Shapiro, Dry). Strong forms:
- A before → after result with units: "44% → 98% triage accuracy", "2 hrs → 3 min per validated workflow" (Vstorm)
- A named quote with a number: "doubled our throughput" (Test Double, attributed to a named client)
- Public work: open-source libraries, a playbook (thoughtbot), a published method

Weak forms: a logo wall without outcomes, "Over 1,000+ positive outcomes", stat tiles with labels but no baseline, case-study titles with no result in them ("Creating Magical Gaming Experiences").

## 7. Process and terms

Commercial terms are the most believable thing a services firm can say, because they're promises you can enforce.
- Leanware: "You pay against shipped milestones you can test, not hours you can't audit."
- Leanware: you "interview and approve each engineer before they join."
- Designjoy publishes its price, and offers "Get 75% back, no questions asked."
- Master of Code's pilot page: "Fixed budget. Fixed timeline."

Write process steps as commitments with an output, not as verbs like Discover / Design / Deliver. Each step should say what the client has in hand at the end of it.

## 8. FAQ

Pull the questions from sales calls. A good set covers:
- price or typical range
- who owns the code and IP
- data handling and security
- who does the work
- timeline to the first useful version
- what happens if it doesn't work
- handoff and ongoing support
- "What if I only have a small project?" (Designjoy asks a version of this)

Answer each in the first sentence, in plain words, then add detail. If the honest answer is "it depends", give the two or three things it depends on and a typical range.

## 9. Closing section

Restate the offer as the next step, with its cost in time and what the reader leaves with. Leanware: "Bring the problem you're working on. Leave with a clear next step." Offer one non-call route for people who don't want a call, for example Designjoy's "Prefer to email?" or Clearleft's "Email Rich about your design challenge."

## 10. Forms and microcopy

- Form length: ask only for what the first conversation needs. Make budget optional, or show ranges.
- Say how long the form takes. thoughtbot: "usually takes less than 5 minutes."
- A good form question: "What does success look like for your team?" (thoughtbot).
- After submit, say who will reply, how, and roughly when. Only state a timeframe the client confirms.
- Errors should say what to fix, in human terms.
